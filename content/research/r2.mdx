**Status:** Current hands-on focus (2026)

## The Question

Which deep learning architectures actually work for time series forecasting
— and how much of their performance comes from the model versus the way you
treat the data? Unlike language or vision, the time series world has no
settled winner: linear models, recurrent networks, and transformer variants
all remain competitive, and results depend heavily on horizon, seasonality,
and data volume.

## My Testbed

My ongoing multivariate forecasting project at TU Darmstadt is where I put
these questions to the test: rolling 24-step predictions across a 336-hour
horizon, evaluated on a public leaderboard. Long horizons are brutal — error
compounds with every rolled step — which makes architecture and validation
choices very visible, very fast.

## Papers That Shaped My Thinking

- [*Are Transformers Effective for Time Series Forecasting?*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.13504)
  (Zeng et al., 2022) — is about DLinear, a one-layer linear model that
  outperforms elaborate transformers on standard long-horizon benchmarks. It
  taught me that in forecasting, strong simple baselines are not a formality
  — they're often the thing to beat.
- [*Informer: Beyond Efficient Transformer for Long Sequence Time-Series
  Forecasting*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.07436) (Zhou et al., 2021) — is
  about making attention tractable for very long sequences via sparsity. It
  taught me that the quadratic cost of attention is a real, practical
  barrier in time series — and that efficiency and architecture design are
  inseparable here.
- [*A Time Series is Worth 64 Words*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.14730)
  (Nie et al., 2023) — is about PatchTST: patching series into subseries
  tokens with channel independence. It taught me that input representation
  can rescue an architecture that "shouldn't" work — tokenization is a
  modeling decision, not a preprocessing detail.
