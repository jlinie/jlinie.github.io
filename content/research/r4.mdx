**Status:** Active research interest

## The Question

When data is limited, expensive, or imbalanced, augmentation is often the
cheapest way to better generalization. But augmentation is domain-specific:
the crop/flip/rotate playbook from computer vision breaks temporal structure
completely. What does *principled* augmentation look like for time series —
and when does augmentation help versus quietly distort the data
distribution?

## Where I Apply It

Directly in my forecasting project: experimenting with augmentation
strategies for multivariate temporal data, where any transformation has to
respect seasonality, trend, and cross-series relationships. It's a perfect
sandbox, because the leaderboard immediately tells me whether an idea helped
or just felt clever.

## Papers That Shaped My Thinking

- [*Time Series Data Augmentation for Deep Learning: A Survey*](https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.12478)
  (Wen et al., 2020) — is about the full landscape of temporal augmentation:
  jittering, scaling, window warping, frequency-domain methods, and
  generative approaches. It taught me that time series augmentation is a
  design space of its own, not a port of vision techniques.
- [*mixup: Beyond Empirical Risk Minimization*](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.09412)
  (Zhang et al., 2018) — is about training on convex combinations of
  examples and labels. It taught me that augmentation isn't only about
  invariances — it can directly reshape the loss landscape and act as a
  powerful regularizer, which reframed how I think about what augmentation
  is *for*.
